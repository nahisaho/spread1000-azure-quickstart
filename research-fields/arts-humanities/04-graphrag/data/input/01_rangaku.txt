Rangaku (蘭学, "Dutch Learning") was a body of scholarship developed in Japan
during the Edo period (1603–1868), through which the Japanese learned about
Western science and technology by way of Dutch traders permitted to reside on
the artificial island of Dejima in Nagasaki Bay. Because Japan was largely
closed to foreigners under the sakoku (national seclusion) policy from 1639,
the Dutch East India Company (VOC) was the only European power allowed to
maintain trading rights, and Dutch books became almost the sole channel through
which Western learning entered Japan.

Sugita Genpaku (1733–1817), a physician of the Obama domain, is celebrated as
one of the founders of Rangaku medicine. Together with Maeno Ryotaku and
Nakagawa Jun'an, he translated the Dutch anatomical text Ontleedkundige
Tafelen (itself a translation of the German Anatomische Tabellen by Johann
Adam Kulmus) into Japanese, publishing it in 1774 as Kaitai Shinsho
("New Book of Anatomy"). This work is often considered the first complete
Japanese translation of a European medical text, and it demonstrated the
precision of Western anatomical knowledge compared with prevailing
Sino-Japanese medical theory.

Rangaku scholars, known as rangakusha, also studied astronomy, cartography,
military science, chemistry, and physics. Notable figures include Shizuki
Tadao, who translated a Dutch textbook that first introduced Newtonian
mechanics to Japan, and Takano Choei, whose translations and political
commentaries brought him into conflict with the shogunate. By the mid-19th
century, Rangaku had prepared the intellectual ground for the more sweeping
adoption of Western science during the Meiji Restoration.
