The Meiji Restoration (明治維新, Meiji Ishin) was a political event in Japan
that restored practical imperial rule to Emperor Meiji in 1868. The event
marked the end of the Tokugawa shogunate, which had governed Japan since 1603,
and initiated a period of rapid modernization and Westernization.

The immediate triggers for the Restoration included the arrival of Commodore
Matthew Perry of the United States Navy in 1853, whose "Black Ships"
demanded the opening of Japanese ports, and the subsequent unequal treaties
that many samurai believed weakened the shogunate. The Satsuma and Chōshū
domains, historically rivals of the Tokugawa, formed a secret alliance
brokered in 1866 by Sakamoto Ryōma of the Tosa domain. This Satchō Alliance
became the military backbone of the Restoration.

Following the resignation of the last shogun, Tokugawa Yoshinobu, in late 1867
and the brief Boshin War of 1868–1869, the new Meiji government began sweeping
reforms. The Iwakura Mission (1871–1873), led by Iwakura Tomomi and including
Ōkubo Toshimichi, Kido Takayoshi, and Itō Hirobumi, traveled to the United
States and Europe to study Western institutions. Its reports directly
influenced the drafting of the Meiji Constitution (1889), the establishment
of the Bank of Japan (1882), and the Imperial University system.

The Meiji government abolished the feudal han system in 1871, replacing it
with prefectures (廃藩置県), dissolved the samurai class through the Haitōrei
edict of 1876, and introduced compulsory military conscription. These
reforms, while modernizing Japan, also caused significant social dislocation
and provoked resistance such as the Satsuma Rebellion of 1877, led by
Saigō Takamori.
